You convert logic-grid puzzles written as prose into formal notation.

You will be given a puzzle: an introduction and a numbered clue list. Translate
it literally into the fact types below. You are a translator, not a solver.

ABSOLUTE RULES

  - Do NOT solve the puzzle. Do not combine clues, infer anything, or add facts
    that the text does not state. One stated fact becomes one output fact.
  - Do NOT drop anything. A numbered clue often carries several facts ("X is on
    Monday, but not with Y" is two). List every one, under that clue's number.
  - Record what the text says, not the strongest reading of it. If a clue says
    "earlier", use `before`, NOT `immediately_before`, however natural the
    tighter reading feels.
  - Name subjects by the exact member names from the introduction's lists. Copy
    the spelling character for character.
  - Facts stated in the INTRODUCTION about counts, category membership, or
    "each is used once" are structural -- do NOT output them as facts. Only the
    numbered clues become facts.
  - If a clue is genuinely ambiguous, choose the WEAKER reading (the one that
    rules out less).

FACT TYPES

Pairing:
  link(A, B)              A and B belong to the same subject.
  not_link(A, B)          A and B belong to different subjects.
  distinct(A, B, C, ...)  All named are different subjects (pairwise). Use for
                          "three different people" phrasing.

Position (only for the ordered category):
  before(A, B)                A is strictly earlier than B, by any amount.
  immediately_before(A, B)    B is exactly one step after A. Direction matters.
  adjacent(A, B)              One step apart, direction unspecified.
  between(A, B, C)            A is strictly between B and C; B and C are in no
                              particular order. A is the MIDDLE one -- put it
                              first.
  gap(A, B, k, rel)           The positions differ by k. rel is "eq" (exactly),
                              "ge" (at least) or "le" (at most). "three apart"
                              and "three later" are both k=3. "three in
                              between" is k=4.
  parity(A, B, same)          same=true: both at odd-numbered or both at
                              even-numbered positions. same=false: one of each.
  arith(A, B, k, op)          Numeric: op="add" means A's value = B's value + k
                              (A is larger). op="mul" means A's value = B's
                              value x k.

Compound (each holds simple facts only, never another compound):
  not(F)                  F is false. Prefer not_link over not(link(...)).
  or(F1, F2, ...)         At least one holds. Inclusive -- possibly several.
  not_both(F1, F2)        At most one holds. Neither holding is allowed too.
  if_then(F1, F2)         If F1 holds then F2 holds. Says nothing when F1 is
                          false. Does NOT assert the converse. Put the
                          condition first.

OUTPUT

Fill `args` with the member names in the order shown above. Use `sub` for the
facts inside a compound, and leave it null otherwise. Leave `k`, `rel`, `op`
and `same` null unless the fact type uses them. Also report the categories and
their members exactly as the introduction lists them.

Convert this puzzle into formal notation.

Preserves, and the Preservation of Grudges

The preserves tent is the most fiercely contested building at the county show, and the only one the stewards bother to lock at night. Six entrants have each set out a single jar for judging, and the committee has priced every jar for the charity sale afterwards. The six prices are $3, $5, $7, $9, $11 and $13, listed here from cheapest to dearest. The six entrants are Drusilla, Harriet, Bernadette, Ottilie, Rafferty and Emeka. The six preserves are sloe and apple butter, medlar jam, hot pepper jelly, damson cheese, quince jelly and rosehip syrup. The six jars are a squat kilner, an unlabelled jar, a recycled honey jar, a jar with a cloth lid, a hexagonal jar and a tall french jar. The six secret ingredients are orange zest, a splash of gin, nothing whatsoever, a spoon of treacle, star anise and a bay leaf.

Each entrant has exactly one price, one preserve, one jar and one secret ingredient, and within each of those four categories every member belongs to a different entrant, so each price, preserve, jar and secret ingredient is used exactly once. Tea is served at four, whatever the judges decide.

Clues:
1. The entrant who uses a recycled honey jar is cheaper than $11 in price order, and the entrant who enters medlar jam is cheaper than $11 in price order.
2. The entrant priced at $3 is exactly one price step cheaper than the entrant who uses a jar with a cloth lid, and the entrant priced at $5 is exactly one price step cheaper than Rafferty.
3. The entrant who enters hot pepper jelly is exactly one price step cheaper than the entrant who adds a spoon of treacle, and Emeka is likewise exactly one price step cheaper than the entrant who adds a spoon of treacle.
4. The entrant who uses an unlabelled jar is exactly one price step apart from the entrant who enters sloe and apple butter, and also exactly one price step apart from the entrant who adds a spoon of treacle.
5. The entrant who enters rosehip syrup and the entrant priced at $11 are exactly one price step apart.
6. The entrant who uses a hexagonal jar is cheaper than the entrant who enters damson cheese in price order.
7. In price order, the entrant who enters hot pepper jelly falls somewhere between Drusilla and the entrant who uses a squat kilner, and the entrant who adds orange zest also falls somewhere between those same two.
8. The entrant who uses a squat kilner is priced exactly $2 more than the entrant who adds orange zest.
9. Ottilie's price is exactly three times the price of the entrant who adds star anise.
10. At most one of the following two things is true: the entrant who enters hot pepper jelly and the entrant who adds star anise are exactly one price step apart, or the entrant who adds a splash of gin and the entrant priced at $13 are exactly one price step apart. They are never both true, and it is quite possible that neither is.
11. If Harriet is dearer than $11 in price order, then the entrant who uses a hexagonal jar and the entrant who enters medlar jam are exactly one price step apart.
12. If the entrant who enters sloe and apple butter and the entrant who adds a splash of gin are exactly one price step apart, then the entrant who enters hot pepper jelly and the entrant priced at $9 are exactly one price step apart.
13. If the entrant who uses a tall french jar and the entrant who adds a bay leaf are exactly one price step apart, then Rafferty is the entrant who adds a bay leaf.
14. If Rafferty is cheaper than the entrant who adds orange zest in price order, then the entrant who uses a jar with a cloth lid and the entrant who adds orange zest are exactly one price step apart.
