You convert logic-grid puzzles written as prose into formal notation.

You will be given a puzzle: an introduction and a numbered clue list. Translate
it literally into the fact types below. You are a translator, not a solver.

ABSOLUTE RULES

  - Do NOT solve the puzzle. Do not combine clues, infer anything, or add facts
    that the text does not state. One stated fact becomes one output fact.
  - Do NOT drop anything. A numbered clue often carries several facts ("X is on
    Monday, but not with Y" is two). List every one, under that clue's number.
  - Record what the text says, not the strongest reading of it. If a clue says
    "earlier", use `before`, NOT `immediately_before`, however natural the
    tighter reading feels.
  - Name subjects by the exact member names from the introduction's lists. Copy
    the spelling character for character.
  - Facts stated in the INTRODUCTION about counts, category membership, or
    "each is used once" are structural -- do NOT output them as facts. Only the
    numbered clues become facts.
  - If a clue is genuinely ambiguous, choose the WEAKER reading (the one that
    rules out less).

FACT TYPES

Pairing:
  link(A, B)              A and B belong to the same subject.
  not_link(A, B)          A and B belong to different subjects.
  distinct(A, B, C, ...)  All named are different subjects (pairwise). Use for
                          "three different people" phrasing.

Position (only for the ordered category):
  before(A, B)                A is strictly earlier than B, by any amount.
  immediately_before(A, B)    B is exactly one step after A. Direction matters.
  adjacent(A, B)              One step apart, direction unspecified.
  between(A, B, C)            A is strictly between B and C; B and C are in no
                              particular order. A is the MIDDLE one -- put it
                              first.
  gap(A, B, k, rel)           The positions differ by k. rel is "eq" (exactly),
                              "ge" (at least) or "le" (at most). "three apart"
                              and "three later" are both k=3. "three in
                              between" is k=4.
  parity(A, B, same)          same=true: both at odd-numbered or both at
                              even-numbered positions. same=false: one of each.
  arith(A, B, k, op)          Numeric: op="add" means A's value = B's value + k
                              (A is larger). op="mul" means A's value = B's
                              value x k.

Compound (each holds simple facts only, never another compound):
  not(F)                  F is false. Prefer not_link over not(link(...)).
  or(F1, F2, ...)         At least one holds. Inclusive -- possibly several.
  not_both(F1, F2)        At most one holds. Neither holding is allowed too.
  if_then(F1, F2)         If F1 holds then F2 holds. Says nothing when F1 is
                          false. Does NOT assert the converse. Put the
                          condition first.

OUTPUT

Fill `args` with the member names in the order shown above. Use `sub` for the
facts inside a compound, and leave it null otherwise. Leave `k`, `rel`, `op`
and `same` null unless the fact type uses them. Also report the categories and
their members exactly as the introduction lists them.

Convert this puzzle into formal notation.

The Orchid House Reckoning

Five growers share the orchid house at Fenn Park, and each is quietly certain that the other four are overwatering. Each grower brings in a single harvest, and the five harvest months, listed from earliest to latest, are January, February, March, April and May. The growers are Nkiruka, Ambrose, Columbine, Rowena and Taavi. The prize plants are a slipper orchid, a dancing lady, a vanilla vine, a jewel orchid and a moth orchid. The potting mixes in use are bark chip, perlite and charcoal, coconut husk, tree fern fibre and sphagnum moss. The pests complained of are mealybug, scale insects, thrips, fungus gnats and root rot. Within each of these categories every item belongs to a different grower, so each harvest month, each grower, each orchid, each potting mix and each pest is used exactly once. From the clues below, work out who grows what, in which mix, troubled by which pest, and in which month the harvest comes.

Clues:
1. Either Rowena and the grower who pots in sphagnum moss harvest two months apart, or the grower who grows a vanilla vine harvests in February, and possibly both.
2. Either the grower who grows a jewel orchid harvests later than May, or Columbine pots in tree fern fibre, and possibly both.
3. Ambrose harvests in the month immediately before March.
4. The grower who is troubled by thrips harvests exactly one month apart from January, in one direction or the other.
5. The grower who is troubled by fungus gnats harvests in the month immediately after April.
6. The grower who grows a slipper orchid harvests exactly one month apart from January, in one direction or the other.
7. The grower who grows a vanilla vine and the grower who is troubled by thrips harvest exactly one month apart, in one direction or the other.
8. The grower who pots in coconut husk and the grower who is troubled by root rot harvest two months apart.
9. The grower who grows a moth orchid harvests earlier than the grower who pots in sphagnum moss.
10. Either the grower who pots in bark chip and the grower who grows a dancing lady harvest three months apart, or the grower who grows a slipper orchid is troubled by fungus gnats, and possibly both.
11. Nkiruka and the grower who grows a vanilla vine harvest exactly one month apart, in one direction or the other.
12. The grower who pots in coconut husk and the grower who is troubled by scale insects harvest four months apart.
